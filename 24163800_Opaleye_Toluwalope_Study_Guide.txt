A. CONVERSATIONAL SCRIPT (about 7-8 minutes spoken)
======================================================================

What did we do and how did we do it?

The short version: I built an auditable benchmarking framework that
measures how well different verification signals survive the everyday
transformations an image goes through online - resizing, JPEG
recompression, filters, cropping, rotation and padding. I took 1,200
deterministically selected images from the MS-COCO 2017 validation
split, applied twelve transform families across eighty named conditions,
and ran four pipelines over them: TrustMark, a neural watermark; two
deliberately simple classical baselines, LSB and DCT; and eight
perceptual hash variants, including the 256-bit PDQ. That produced
768,000 hash rows and 97,200 rows per watermark method - the 1,200
untransformed baselines plus 96,000 transformed observations. On top of
that benchmark I built my own contribution: a hybrid two-layer recovery
channel, where if TrustMark fails to decode, a second, BCH-coded DCT
fallback watermark gets a chance to rescue the payload. And I wrapped
it in a dashboard so you can sign and verify an image and see each
method's answer side by side.

Why did we do it?

Because provenance claims are easy to overstate. When a platform
recompresses or crops an image, the metadata is stripped, the pixels
change and the cryptographic digest breaks - yet the image is still
recognisably the same image. Four different questions keep getting
blurred together in the literature: is this image similar to a
reference, can I recover a hidden message, is it byte-for-byte intact,
and who signed it. I wanted to separate similarity, recovery, integrity
and accountability, and measure honestly where each signal lives and
where it dies. There is also a professional angle: if a verification
score is misread as an authenticity guarantee, you get unjustified
takedowns or misplaced trust, so the whole framework is built around
evidence discipline - every number in the report traces back to a
validated file.

Research question

To what extent do a neural watermark, perceptual hashes and classical
watermarking baselines retain interpretable verification signal when
images undergo graduated photometric, compression and geometric
transformations - and how should that signal be reported without
confusing recovery, similarity, integrity or provenance?

Methodology

Everything runs through one shared module, transforms.py, the single
source of truth for the twelve families and eighty conditions. Random
operations like random crop and salt-and-pepper noise derive their seeds
deterministically from the image ID, transform name and intensity via
SHA-256, so any machine can reconstruct the exact same transformed
image. JPEG is genuinely written to disk and reopened, so we test the
real codec, not a simulation. Ten validation gates guard the data -
coverage, duplicate keys, ranges - and a run that fails validation is
not reported. For uncertainty, the image is the statistical unit:
percentile bootstrap with 2,000 resamples at the fixed seed 20260812,
and Wilson intervals for decode proportions. Why that seed? A bootstrap
is a random resampling procedure, so if you do not pin the seed your
confidence intervals wiggle on every rerun. 20260812 is an arbitrary but
public constant - essentially the date the analysis was frozen, 12
August 2026 - recorded in the output metadata so anyone regenerates
identical intervals.

Results

The headline: the hybrid two-layer channel lifts exact payload recovery
from 60.7 percent with TrustMark alone to 67.3 percent over the 96,000
transformed observations - a 6.6 percentage point gain. The rescue is
concentrated where I predicted: dimension-preserving conditions and
low-quality JPEG. Table 12 is the money table, the JPEG quality ladder.
At quality 5 TrustMark alone recovers just 2.7 percent of payloads; the
two-layer system gets 69.7 percent. At quality 20 it is 42.3 up to 93.1
percent. Why show those two qualities? Quality 5 is the extreme stress
case where the fallback's complementary strength is most dramatic, and
quality 20 is a realistic aggressive recompression you would actually
see on social platforms. Between them sits quality 10, where recovery
dips to 32.2 percent - an irregularity we report openly as codec-
dependent non-monotonic behaviour rather than hiding it. Every recovery
figure is drawn on a full 0-to-100 percent scale, a deliberate honesty
choice: recovery is a proportion, and a truncated axis would make the
6.6-point gain look enormous. The full scale shows the gain is real but
modest, and that geometric attacks still beat us - rotation, cropping
and letterbox remain near chance, with letterbox two-layer at only 10.4
percent, because the fallback's block layout assumes dimensions
survived. On the hash side, the pooled bit-error fraction is 7.4
percent, and PDQ being 256-bit matters: the other hashes are 64-bit, so
distances are normalised by bit length before comparison - and we had
to fix a real serialisation bug where PDQ was stored as multi-character
strings per dimension, which silently corrupted the distances. One more
honest result: my interim proposal hypothesised over 90 percent recovery
across JPEG and PNG. The measured JPEG figure is 85.6 percent, PNG was
never implemented, and that is reported as a non-confirmation, not a
hit.

Brief summary

The framework works, it is auditable, and it is honest. Neural
watermarking and perceptual hashes provide complementary signals under
most routine transforms; the two-layer hybrid buys a real, measured
6.6-point recovery improvement; geometric transformations remain the
open frontier; and every claim is bounded by what the validated
1,200-image benchmark can actually support.

B. CODE PRIDE LIST (5 items)
======================================================================

1. Deterministic seeding in transforms.py (make_seed). Stochastic
   transforms seed their RNG from SHA-256 of image_id + transform name +
   intensity, deliberately avoiding Python's built-in hash(), which is
   randomised between processes. That one design decision is what makes
   the entire 96,000-row benchmark reproducible on any machine, and it
   is why the report can promise row-level traceability.

2. Hard validation gates in validate_experiment.py. Before any analysis,
   the script recomputes expected coverage (image count times conditions
   per image), counts unique image IDs, and detects duplicate composite
   keys - then calls SystemExit on any mismatch. A 100-image command
   literally cannot validate 1,200-image coverage, so a partial run can
   never masquerade as a final result. That is the G2/G3 gate story in
   one file.

3. The fallback watermark channel in fallback_watermark.py. Each payload
   bit is BCH_SUPER-coded into a 100-bit packet and repeated five times
   in interleaved 8x8 DCT blocks spread evenly across the image, so a
   partial crop destroys at most a fraction of the repeats; decoding is
   majority vote before BCH correction, and all blocks are transformed
   in one vectorised batch call. Dimension-changing transforms fail
   cleanly by design rather than decoding against a mismatched layout -
   that is thoughtful failure engineering, not just happy-path code.

4. Reproducible statistics in analyze_uncertainty.py. The bootstrap uses
   a fixed local RNG seeded at 20260812 (not global numpy state, so no
   other code can perturb the stream), decode proportions get exact
   Wilson binomial intervals, the CLI refuses fewer than 100 resamples,
   and the script self-tests against known values. Statistics you can
   rerun and defend.

5. Honest, defensive demo engineering in streamlit_app.py (archive.zip).
   The feature pipeline is byte-for-byte identical to training (same
   TF-IDF vectorizer, scaler and column order, with a comment explaining
   why that is essential); classify_url_field() detects whether the URL
   column is a real link, a binary flag or a hash placeholder instead of
   assuming the schema; missing dates become a -1 sentinel rather than
   crashing; models load once via st.cache_resource; and the example
   buttons write st.session_state before the widgets render, with a
   comment explaining Streamlit's rerun semantics. It even ships an
   honest disclaimer that the prototype is not a substitute for
   professional security software.


======================================================================
C. CHALLENGE MAP (5 items)
======================================================================

1. Raw-bit accuracy required reaching inside TrustMark's decoder before
   error correction. The obvious metric - exact decoded-message success
   - hides partial signal, so the pipeline had to tap the raw decoder
   output upstream of ECC (gate G5). Getting that measurement right
   changed the numbers: TrustMark's mean raw bit accuracy is 85.17%
   while its exact decode rate is 60.73%.

2. The PDQ serialisation bug. PDQ is a 256-bit hash but was serialised
   as a multi-character representation per dimension instead of one bit,
   silently inflating every Hamming distance. Finding it required
   actually auditing hash lengths against the spec (gate G4) and
   recomputing the affected hash output - a measurement-validity fix,
   not a cosmetic one.

3. Guaranteeing 1,200 images x 80 conditions = 96,000 transformed rows
   per watermark method (768,000 hash rows) with zero duplicates and
   zero silent baseline mixing. Rows from the same image are dependent,
   baselines are encode-only and must be excluded from robustness means,
   and the compressed hash CSV needs integrity-checked reading - all
   enforced through coverage and duplicate-key gates rather than trust.

4. The quality-10 JPEG irregularity. Recovery does not degrade
   monotonically: at quality 10 the fallback (29.4%) underperforms its
   own quality-5 result (68.9%), a codec-quantisation interaction.
   Methodologically hard because the honest move is to report the
   anomaly and decline to smooth it away - first-crossing-only reporting
   would have concealed it.

5. A falsified hypothesis, handled honestly. The interim proposal
   promised above-90% payload recovery across JPEG and PNG; the measured
   two-layer JPEG figure is 85.6% (TrustMark alone 63.5%) and PNG was
   never implemented. The hard part was methodological - reporting a
   non-confirmation with the same prominence as the wins, and reframing
   the contribution as an evidence-bounded framework rather than a
   robustness claim.
